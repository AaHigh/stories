Meta Ordered to Pay $375 Million Over Child Safety Violations

A New Mexico jury found that the company misled consumers about the safety of Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp and enabled the sexual exploitation of young users. One of Meta’s first major courtroom losses on these claims.

In one of the company’s first major losses on child-safety claims, a New Mexico jury on March 24, 2026, found that Meta Platforms had misled consumers about the safety of its platforms and enabled the sexual exploitation of young users. Jurors ordered Meta to pay $375 million in civil penalties under the state’s Unfair Practices Act.

What the Jury Decided

After a nearly seven-week trial in Santa Fe, the 12-person jury found Meta liable on every count brought by the state. They determined the company willfully violated New Mexico’s consumer protection laws by making false or misleading statements about platform safety and by engaging in unconscionable trade practices that took advantage of children’s vulnerability.

Jurors calculated 37,500 violations and applied the maximum statutory penalty of $5,000 for each, producing the $375 million total. The state had sought closer to $2 billion based on a higher estimate of affected teenagers.

“The jury’s verdict is a historic victory for every child and family who has paid the price for Meta’s choice to put profits over kids’ safety. Meta executives knew their products harmed children, disregarded warnings from their own employees and lied to the public about what they knew.”
— Raúl Torrez, New Mexico Attorney General

How the Case Began

Attorney General Torrez sued Meta in 2023. State investigators created decoy Facebook and Instagram accounts that appeared to belong to children under 14. Those accounts were quickly flooded with sexually explicit material and solicitation messages from adults. The investigation led to arrests, including two men apprehended at a motel where they believed they were meeting a 12-year-old girl.

Prosecutors argued Meta had long known its platforms were used by predators to contact minors, yet failed to implement effective safeguards and continued to present the products as safe for young users and their parents. Internal documents presented at trial showed the company had received warnings from its own employees about child sexual exploitation risks.

Meta’s Position

Meta has said it will appeal the verdict. The company has long maintained that it invests heavily in safety tools, removes large volumes of violating content, and never guaranteed that its platforms would be completely free of bad actors. During the trial, Meta argued that the state’s undercover investigation was flawed and that it has been transparent about the residual risks on social networks.

Shareholders largely shrugged at the news. Meta’s market value sat near $1.5 trillion at the time; the $375 million figure represents a fraction of a single day’s trading volume for the company.

Why This Verdict Matters

This was the first jury verdict of its kind against Meta on claims that the company enabled child sexual exploitation and misled the public about safety. It arrives amid a broader wave of litigation by states, school districts, and individual plaintiffs accusing social media companies of designing addictive products that harm young users’ mental health and of failing to protect children from predators.

A separate California case involving Meta and YouTube reached a jury verdict the following day, finding the companies liable for addictive design features that harmed a young plaintiff. That jury awarded a combined $6 million. Additional state cases seeking far larger sums remain pending.

For communities focused on public safety and the protection of minors, the New Mexico outcome supplies a concrete data point: a jury of ordinary citizens examined the evidence and concluded that Meta’s practices crossed the legal line under state consumer-protection law.

High Tower Note

Child sexual exploitation is not an abstract policy debate. It is a concrete public-safety failure that reaches every community, including Fresno and the Tower District. When platforms design systems that make contact between predators and minors frictionless, and then represent those systems as safe, the cost is measured in real harm to real children. A jury has now put a dollar figure—and a formal finding of deception—on that practice. The appeal will test whether the finding holds. The underlying evidence of what occurs on these platforms will remain.
