Olive Avenue Dawn

The underground tax is ending. The bicycle light is already here. Love for people and dogs does not require poverty.

There is a kind of person who smiles when the price of gas goes up for everyone else. Not because they studied the chemistry. Not because they ran the numbers on what imported oil actually costs the planet. They smile because the rule they wrote in the dark made the ordinary life of the ordinary person harder, and that hardness feels, to them, like proof that they still matter.

Call them predators of public safety if you like. They do not hunt with teeth. They hunt with process-skipping. They write the policy first, deny it under oath for two years, then admit the policy only after the lawsuit has already burned through the calendar. The Kern County judge called the result what it was: an invalid underground regulation. Adopted without the rulemaking the Administrative Procedure Act requires. Arbitrary. Unsupported by the evidence the agency itself had already collected.

That is Criminal culture. Not the street kind that steals a catalytic converter at 2 a.m. The kind that seizes the machinery of the state, treats the law as optional, and then lectures the people who still have to buy the gas. Impunity as a personality. Addiction to power dressed up as climate virtue. The same addiction that leaves the rest of us paying more for everything that moves, heats, or gets grown.

The Joy They Take

Watch the face when someone points out that the agency’s own record showed the ban would raise global emissions by forcing California to import more oil. The predator does not look embarrassed. The predator looks pleased. The point was never the emissions. The point was the prohibition itself, the feeling of having closed a door that other people still need to walk through. Public safety is not the product. The destruction of the ordinary person’s options is the product. That is what produces the satisfaction.

Kyle Kirkland has spent years doing the opposite work. Rescuing animals that other people had already decided were disposable. Building businesses that employ people instead of explaining to them why employment is no longer possible. The man who chairs the Fresno Chaffee Zoo and runs a foundation that has pulled thousands of dogs and cats back from the edge does not smile when a regulation makes life more expensive. He asks why the regulation was allowed to skip the process the law already wrote down. That is the difference between someone who loves living things and someone who loves the feeling of having stopped something.

The Avenue That Already Chose Differently

Between the Circle K on Olive and Ragin’ Records the light is already different. You can ride a bicycle without treating the car as a moral failure. You can walk a dog without explaining yourself to a committee. The shops are small enough that the person behind the counter still knows which records came in yesterday. The economy there is not theoretical. It is the coffee, the vinyl, the late-night snacks, the people who decided the stretch of Avenue could stay human even while Sacramento was busy inventing new ways to make energy illegal.

This is the interim that does not require permission. The bicycle-friendly Tower District does not wait for a perfect energy policy. It simply refuses to treat the car as the only remaining option while the underground rules are still being litigated. People ditch the car for the short trip because the short trip is pleasant, not because a bureaucrat declared the car a sin. Dogs get walked. Records get flipped. The cost of living has not yet fallen, but the cost of living well for an hour has already dropped to the price of a walk and a leash.

What the Dawn Looks Like

The future is not complicated. Produce more of what California already has. Follow the process the Administrative Procedure Act already wrote. Stop treating the denial of a permit as a personality trait. When the price of energy falls because the oil is no longer required to travel halfway around the world under someone else’s rules, the price of everything else begins to follow. Housing, food, the tank of gas that still gets used for the longer trip. Poverty is not a permanent climate condition. It is, in large part, the accumulated result of rules that were never allowed to be tested against evidence.

On Olive Avenue the shining is already visible. The stretch between Circle K and Ragin’ Records does not need a new slogan. It needs the rest of the state to stop punishing the production that makes the rest of life cheaper. Dogs will still need walks. People will still need records and late-night snacks and the chance to sit outside without calculating whether the trip is worth the gas. Love for animals and love for neighbors is not a substitute for energy. It is what becomes easier once the underground tax is finally struck down.

The predators will keep smiling for a while. They always do. The smile is the tell. The rest of us can keep walking the Avenue, riding the bike, and refusing to treat Criminal culture as normal. The process exists. The light on Olive already proves what the Avenue looks like when the process is allowed to matter again.

A Note for the Group

This is not a campaign ad. It is a reminder that the same people who love dogs and neighbors are allowed to notice when the rules are being ignored. Kyle Kirkland has spent years on the animal side of that love. The Tower District has spent years on the human side. Neither side requires us to accept underground regulations as the price of living here. The cost of living can come down. The Avenue can stay kind. Those two things are not in conflict.
