The $16 Million Flip

A developer bought a senior home in Cheviot Hills for $11.2 million, then sold it to a homeless-services nonprofit for $27.3 million — with your money. A federal case, a judge's finding of probable fraud, and a building that still hasn't housed a single person. The viral post about it is mostly right. Here is what it gets wrong, and why the corrections matter.

By Aaron Michael Hightower November 11, 2026 Los Angeles, California

You have probably seen the post by now. It has been passed around Facebook, Threads, and X for weeks: a reality TV star exposes insane corruption in a Los Angeles homeless project — a $30 million grant, a senior home cleared of old people, a building listed at $11.2 million and sold for $27 million, $400,000 a bed a year for rooms that sit empty. It ends with a demand for prison sentences for every Democrat involved. I checked every line of it against the record. The core of it is real, and it is worse than the post makes it sound in one way — a federal judge has already found probable fraud. But three of its details are wrong, and the wrong details are the ones that would let the people who actually did this walk away while the argument stays about the wrong names.

Chapter OneThe deal

May 2023 to April 2024: a middleman, a markup, and a missing appraisal

The building is the former Terraza of Cheviot Hills, a 76-unit assisted-living facility at 3340 Shelby Drive in Cheviot Hills. In May 2023, under the banner of a declared homelessness emergency, the City of Los Angeles and the Weingart Center Association — one of the city's largest homeless-services nonprofits, headquartered in Skid Row — were negotiating a Project Homekey deal for it. The man on the other side of the table was developer Steven Taylor. He did not own the building yet. He did not open escrow until June 16, 2023.

Taylor bought it for $11.2 million. Federal prosecutors say he used fake bank statements to obtain the loans and lines of credit to do it. Then he sold it to Weingart for $27.3 million — more than double, months later, with no improvements in between. The markup was $16.1 million, and it was paid with public money: up to $20.5 million from the City of Los Angeles and $26.6 million in state Homekey funds, with a $1.4-million developer fee going to Weingart itself. When acquisition, operations, and renovation costs are counted, the total public commitment approaches $60 million — roughly $800,000 per bed, for 76 units.

Spencer Pratt, who has been hammering this deal in videos since January 2026, says the city ran a competitive Homekey process with more than 30 bidders and then swapped the site at the last minute to favor Weingart, with no appraisal in place when the deal was approved. That part is Pratt's account, and I am labeling it as his. What the record proves on its own is the price gap, and the gap is the scandal: a judge has already put a number on it.

Chapter TwoThe seniors

A functioning assisted-living facility, emptied on a deadline

Before it was a line item, 3340 Shelby Drive was a home. It housed senior residents — a working assisted-living facility, not a vacant lot. To make the deal happen, the building had to be delivered vacant, and the purchase agreement required it. Residents were given 60 days to leave or appear in court. A tenant replacement plan was filed to secure the funding, describing protections that the purchase agreement itself made impossible. Eviction records contradict the official story about when and how the residents were relocated.

That is the part of this story that should sit with you the longest. The fraud numbers are abstract. The seniors are not. People who had a home were moved out of it so a building could be sold twice — and the people it was sold to house have never moved in. The grant agreement required the building to be fully occupied by February 2025. That deadline came and went. As of this writing, the building has yet to open, and not one homeless person has been housed there.

Chapter ThreeThe judge, the audits, and the woman with 7,500 records requests

What the courts and the paperwork already say

Residents fought back the only way residents can: they filed a taxpayer lawsuit under California Code of Civil Procedure §526a. Los Angeles Superior Court Judge James C. Chalfant looked at the purchase and found what judges rarely say out loud at this stage:

"Prima facie evidence of excessive payment, waste, and probably fraud." — Judge James C. Chalfant, Los Angeles Superior Court, on the Shelby Drive purchase; the court separately put the probable taxpayer waste at about $16 million

In June 2026, the court rejected Weingart's attempt to dismiss the fraud and waste claims. The case is moving toward discovery.

The paper trail behind the deal is just as bad as the price. Weingart has been out of compliance with federally required annual audits. Its most recent audit, submitted in July 2025 for fiscal year 2023, did not disclose more than $50 million in federally funded Homekey grants, and a 2023 audit found the nonprofit repeatedly failed to properly document its cash flows. In November 2025, Weingart placed its two top officials on leave — president and CEO Kevin Murray, a former state senator, and Ben Rosen, its director of real estate development — and hired an outside law firm to review property valuations. Murray has since resigned from the Los Angeles County Affordable Housing Solutions Agency board, the body that allocates Measure A sales-tax money for housing. He was appointed to that board by Mayor Karen Bass.

None of this was uncovered by an inspector general. It was uncovered by a resident — Samantha, of the Integrity Project — who filed more than 7,500 public records requests and pieced the deal together document by document. Remember that the next time someone tells you the system polices itself.

And the criminal case: in August 2025, federal authorities arrested Steven Taylor. He faces nine felony counts of bank fraud and money laundering, is free on a $3.6 million bond, and maintains his innocence. Federal prosecutors have said they are investigating what the City of Los Angeles and Weingart knew about his actions. The investigation, led by the U.S. Attorney's Office, is still open. To be exact: Taylor is the only person charged. No charges have been filed against Weingart, against Mayor Bass, against Nithya Raman, or against Kevin Murray. A demand for prison sentences is a demand, not a verdict, and this story will not present it as one.

Chapter FourWhat the viral post gets wrong

Five corrections. Each one matters, because each wrong name is a right name getting away.

Correction one — the $30 million grant

The viral post says Weingart got a $30 million grant for this property. The $30 million figure belongs to a different project that never happened: Weingart planned to buy a 122-room Extended Stay America hotel in Torrance for $30 million with Homekey+ funds. Independent experts valued that hotel at $21.5 to $22.7 million; Torrance's own appraisal said $10.2 million. Weingart pulled out in August 2025. For Shelby Drive, the state money was $26.6 million in Homekey funds, plus up to $20.5 million from the city. Same pattern, different building — and if you cite the wrong building, the defense writes you off.

Correction two — "the LA County Board of Supervisors (all Democrats)"

Wrong twice. Supervisor Kathryn Barger, of the Fifth District, is a Republican — the only Republican on the five-member board. And the county board didn't fund Shelby Drive at all. The county's $12 million was committed to the Torrance hotel project, the one that died. The Shelby money was city and state money. Blaming the county board for this deal is blaming people who didn't touch it.

Correction three — former Mayor Eric Garcetti

Garcetti left office in December 2022. The Shelby deal was negotiated in 2023 and closed in April 2024, under Mayor Karen Bass. Whatever Garcetti's record on homelessness spending, he was gone before this deal existed. He does not belong on this list.

Correction four — $400,000 per bed, per year

This is Spencer Pratt's allegation, and it has not been confirmed through public contract documents. What the documents do support is the all-in math: roughly $60 million in public money committed for 76 units — about $800,000 per bed, once — for a building that has housed nobody. That number is damning enough without an annual rate nobody has produced the contract for.

Correction five — "no obligation to house anyone"

Also wrong, and the truth is worse. The grant agreement required the building to be fully occupied by February 2025. The obligation existed. The deadline passed, the building stayed empty, and the money stayed spent. The scandal isn't that nobody was required to house anyone. It's that everyone was required to — and nobody did.

One more, for completeness: the post says Weingart operates "around 10 similar facilities." The Los Angeles Times puts it higher — Weingart operates or is developing more than a dozen housing projects across L.A. County, serving nearly 2,000 people a day. The post understates the footprint. That is the only direction in which it understates anything.

And the scale behind all of it: for the 2025–26 fiscal year, Los Angeles approved $953.3 million for homelessness programs, a total that pushed past $1 billion with later additions. By March 31, the city had spent or committed just $368.9 million of it. That left $636.1 million unspent or uncommitted — while 45,194 people were counted homeless in the city, 29,115 of them unsheltered. Raman vowed to track every homelessness dollar, fill empty shelter beds, and cut encampments by at least half before the 2028 Olympics. She will be judged against the same books she spent three years chairing the committee over.

Chapter SixThe man who wouldn't let it go

Spencer Pratt lost his house. Then he ran for mayor on this deal.

Spencer Pratt is the former MTV reality star from The Hills. In January 2025 he lost his Pacific Palisades home in the Palisades fire, and he has spent the year since accusing the city's leadership of criminal negligence — first over the fire, then over the money. His January 20, 2026 video laying out the Shelby deal has been watched and reposted for months; he took the same case to Joe Rogan's podcast in April. He ran for mayor of Los Angeles in the June 2026 primary and finished third with about 25.8 percent of the vote — more than 200,000 people — behind Bass and Raman. He has vowed to hand the matter to the IRS and the DOJ. A federal investigation is already underway, under the U.S. Attorney's Office, and the developer at the center of it has already been charged. Pratt didn't start the investigation. He made it impossible to ignore.

There is a Fresno parallel here, and it's the reason I'm writing this. I have spent the last month investigating a local page that I believe monetizes suffering while the people it claims to serve stay exactly where they are. The pattern is the same in both cities: the money moves, the buildings don't open, the people who point at it get called the problem, and the only person who did the homework was a private citizen with a records request. In Los Angeles, her name is Samantha. In Fresno, the homework is still mine to do. The difference between the two cities is that in Los Angeles, a judge has already said the word fraud out loud.

ClosingWhat honest would look like

Honest would be the Shelby building opening its doors to the people it was bought for — or the money coming back. Honest would be the taxpayer lawsuit reaching discovery, with depositions, so the public learns who knew the $11.2 million price when they approved $27.3 million. Honest would be Weingart filing the audits the law already requires, disclosing the Homekey money it took, and answering for the $50 million its own audit left out.

And honest would be getting the names right. Karen Bass moved this money. Kevin Murray took it. Nithya Raman chaired the committee that oversaw it, and now she owns the cleanup. Eric Garcetti and the county board did not do this one. A list with the wrong names on it is a gift to the right names — it lets them point at the errors and ignore the $16 million. The flip is real. The fraud finding is real. The empty building is real. Don't hand the people responsible a reason to dismiss all three by getting the easy parts wrong.

One building. Seventy-six rooms. Zero people housed. Sixteen million dollars that a judge says cannot be justified by market value. That is the story. Everything else is commentary — including mine.

— Aaron Michael Hightower, from the High Tower District
